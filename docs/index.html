Detecting and diagnosing faults in a chemical plant

How effective are different machine learning models at detecting and diagnosing faults in the Tennessee Eastman Process from instantaneous process measurements, and how quickly can a fault be detected?

1 · Two tasks 2 · The process 3 · The data 4 · Labels 5 · How models are judged 6 · Results so far

1Two tasks

Industrial process faults can reduce product quality, disrupt operations and create safety risks. An operator needs to know two things, quickly and reliably.

1 · DetectionIs a fault happening right now? A yes/no answer from a single 3-minute snapshot of the plant, judged on how many faults it catches against how many false alarms it raises. 2 · DiagnosisWhich of the 20 fault types is it? Judged on how evenly well it names every fault (macro-F1), and which faults it confuses.

Instantaneous measurements only. Every model sees one row at a time: the 52 process variables at a single moment, with no history. That keeps the models simple and comparable, and it is also the main limitation: some faults only show up as slow drifts that a single snapshot can't reveal.

2The Tennessee Eastman Process

A realistic simulation of a chemical plant published by Eastman Chemical Company (Downs and Vogel, 1993) and a standard benchmark for process monitoring. Four gaseous reactants (A, C, D, E) react to form two liquid products (G and H) in a plant with five main units: a reactor, a condenser, a vapour-liquid separator, a recycle compressor and a product stripper.

52 variables, recorded every 3 minutes

  • 41 measurements (xmeas_1–xmeas_41): flows, pressures, levels and temperatures (1–22), plus gas compositions from analyzers (23–41).
  • 11 manipulated variables (xmv_1–xmv_11): the valve positions the control system sets.
All 52 variables

20 programmed faults

Steps (sudden changes), random variations, a slow drift, sticking valves, and five faults of unknown type.

4Labels: when is a row "faulty"?

A faulty run starts out normal; the fault is switched on partway through. Rows before that moment are labelled normal, rows after it faulty. Fault-free runs are normal throughout.

A consequence: faulty rows outnumber normal ones roughly ten to one, so a model that always answers "fault" already scores an F1 of about 0.96. That's why this site reports false alarms and recall on normal operation alongside the usual metrics.

5How models are judged

  • Split by simulation run, never by row. Neighbouring rows of one run are nearly identical, so a row-level split would leak answers. Within each fault type, 80% of runs train the models and 20% validate them.
  • Choices are made on validation. Decision thresholds and the best model of each kind are picked using the validation runs only.
  • The test files are used once. The chosen models are retrained on all training runs and evaluated a single time on the separate test files, keeping the thresholds chosen on validation.
  • A common operating point. Detection models are compared at the threshold that gives each one 5% false alarms on normal validation rows.
